18:T16b6,<h1>The Path Between the Seas</h1>
<p><strong>By David McCullough</strong></p>
<p>Imagine a ditch—ten feet deep, seventy feet wide—running the length of India, from Kashmir to Kanyakumari. Or the same trench cut across America, San Francisco to New York. That's the scale of dirt lifted out of fifty miles of tropical mountain-jungle to make the Panama Canal. The numbers are arithmetic; the reality is geological surgery.</p>
<h2>The Early Failures</h2>
<p>Explorers had been humbled for centuries. The Caledonian Bay expedition ended like a candle in the rain. The Darien strip was a green labyrinth of mosquitoes, rot, and fever. Ulysses S. Grant sent men to reconnoiter, and they came back whipped by nature. Even the Panama Railroad, a narrow thread across the isthmus, charged $25 a seat—so costly it was called "the world's most expensive train ride." The stock soared on Wall Street, a fever almost as contagious as malaria.</p>
<p>Nature staged the real blockade. The Atlantic sat flat as glass, while the Pacific rose and dropped twenty feet like a giant breathing. Rain fell like punishment—130 inches a year. The jungle smelled of compost and life gone rank. Clothes soured, furniture collapsed, and mosquitoes carried a deck of diseases they played all year.</p>
<h2>De Lesseps and the French</h2>
<p>Into this swamp came Ferdinand de Lesseps. He wasn't an engineer; he was a diplomat with champagne charm. But he had sliced the desert open at Suez, and for that Paris crowned him "the Great Engineer."</p>
<p>In 1880, he announced Panama would be another trench, sea-level, simple as a plow furrow. The French public bought his dream as if it were theater tickets. Fortunes poured in. Machinery was shipped, men were shipped, even the shovels were shipped.</p>
<p>But Suez had been sand; Panama was stone. At Suez, a trench; at Panama, mountains. Here, oceans themselves didn't match—one flat, one restless. The French engineers came armed with math and pride. They dug, but the mountain laughed. Mudslides erased weeks of work in hours. They chewed one-third of a Suez out of the jungle before the whole thing collapsed in scandal. It was less a canal than a French national wound.</p>
<h2>Roosevelt and the American Turn</h2>
<p>Theodore Roosevelt didn't treat the canal as Columbus's dream. For him, it was the fulcrum of empire. Build the canal and America would pivot a single navy between two oceans.</p>
<p>Nicaragua or Panama? Congress argued. Panama was shorter, cheaper, and partly dug already. And no one wanted to build Nicaragua only to watch another nation carve Panama later. Roosevelt saw the logic. With American gunboats lurking, Panama declared independence from Colombia, and the U.S. secured its canal zone.</p>
<h2>Gorgas and the Mosquito</h2>
<p>The first battle wasn't dirt—it was disease. William C. Gorgas, who had killed yellow fever in Havana, swore mosquitoes carried the plague. Doubters muttered, but Roosevelt stood by him. Gorgas drained swamps, fumigated homes, spread oil on puddles. He turned the isthmus into a war zone where the enemy was a winged speck. Without him, the canal would have been an unmarked grave.</p>
<h2>Stevens: The Railroad Man</h2>
<p>Next came John F. Stevens. Roosevelt's best hire. Stevens saw the project not as a canal, but as a railroad on steroids. He doubled the tracks, laid heavy gauge, bought locomotives until they were practically herds. Dirt went out like coal on a factory belt.</p>
<p>He built first: hospitals, houses, machine shops, sewers. Morale and machinery before mud. At one point, 40,000 men worked under his system, the largest workforce the isthmus had ever seen. "The canal will dig itself," Stevens said. He meant: put the scaffolding in place, and the mountain will fall.</p>
<p>Washington's panel voted 8–5 for sea-level. Stevens stayed quiet at first. But after facing the Chagres River, he changed. A sea-level canal was like trying to cage a tiger with string. Better to use the river's claws than fight them. Locks, not trenches. He went back to Washington and made the case. Roosevelt agreed, swatting aside the panel.</p>
<p>Stevens, worn out, resigned.</p>
<h2>Goethals: The Organizer</h2>
<p>In came George Washington Goethals. At first, men distrusted him. He wasn't Stevens. But Goethals held Sunday court, listening to everything—pay disputes, discipline, marital squabbles, even complaints about baseball pitching. By judging fairly, he earned loyalty.</p>
<p>He reorganized the whole effort like an army campaign: Atlantic Division, Central Division, Pacific Division. He launched a weekly newspaper with excavation scoreboards, and the crews turned into competitive teams. The isthmus became both workplace and sport.</p>
<h2>The Culebra Cut</h2>
<p>The monster was the Culebra Cut. Cutting through the continental divide meant hollowing out a mountain. At one point, seven parallel trains hauled spoil night and day. Rails were torn up and relaid every few days as the excavation dropped deeper. It was efficiency at the edge of madness.</p>
<p>Then the mountain pushed back. Slides in wet season, slides in dry. Digging deeper was like stripping the flying buttresses off a Gothic cathedral—the walls folded inward. Whole sections collapsed as though the earth itself had vetoed the project. The only answer was brute persistence: keep hauling dirt until the mountain grew tired of fighting.</p>
<h2>The Finish</h2>
<p>Finally, the locks rose. Concrete walls so massive they seemed quarried from myth, gates swung not by steam but by electric motors—the first of their kind. The inaugural ship through was French. It was only justice.</p>
<h2>Verdict</h2>
<p>A wonderful book with great descriptions of humans trying a modern Bhagiratha attempt at connecting two oceans.</p>
