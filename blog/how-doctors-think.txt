18:T12ea,<h1>How Doctors Think</h1>
<p><strong>By Jerome Groopman</strong></p>
<p>There's an old Sanskrit line: <em>vaidyo nārāyaṇo hari</em> - the doctor is God on earth. Step into a hospital at your lowest moment, and you can feel the truth of it. Yet, like all of us, doctors are still human. They have empathy, but also blind spots. They feel deeply, but they tire. They carry biases, fall prey to marketing, and occasionally mishear the very words meant to save a life.</p>
<p>Groopman's book isn't a manifesto, and it's not a takedown. It's a kind of field guide—useful to patients, their families, and to doctors themselves—on navigating the messiness of medical thinking. He takes us on rounds: through the eyes of fledgling residents, the chaos of the emergency room, the grind of primary care, the subtlety of pediatrics, the precision of radiology, the dilemmas of cardiology, and the life-and-death stakes of oncology. In each place, the thinking is slightly different. And in each, mistakes are as human as successes.</p>
<h2>The Battle Between Algorithms and Intuition</h2>
<p>We live in an era of checklists, algorithms, and evidence-based protocols. These have saved lives. But when cases are vague or complex, rigid rules fall short. Doctors lean instead on something slipperier—intuition, judgment, a feel for the story. That's where medicine remains an art.</p>
<p>Groopman insists that the real bedrock of medicine isn't MRI machines or blood panels. It's language. The story the patient tells, the pauses between words, the look on their face when they say "it's just a little pain." A doctor who listens well, who asks open questions and catches the unspoken, is far more likely to find the truth.</p>
<h2>How Thinking Goes Wrong</h2>
<p>The book maps out the cognitive traps that ambush doctors:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Representativeness error</strong>: seeing a patient who looks like the "classic" case, and stopping there</li>
<li><strong>Availability bias</strong>: what's top of mind feels most likely</li>
<li><strong>Confirmation bias</strong>: searching only for signs that fit the hunch</li>
<li><strong>Anchoring</strong>: latching onto the first idea and never letting go</li>
<li><strong>Attribution error</strong>: letting stereotypes color the diagnosis</li>
</ul>
<p>These aren't quirks of character—they're built into human thought. But in medicine, the stakes are higher. Most errors in care, Groopman argues, come not from technical mistakes but from flaws in thinking.</p>
<h2>The Demands of Each Stage</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Emergency rooms</strong>: Doctors must recognize patterns instantly, but also cultivate a "studied calm"—deliberately slowing down, asking "what else could it be?"</li>
<li><strong>Primary care</strong>: Sheer patient volume pushes doctors toward rote responses, making it easy to miss the subtle clue</li>
<li><strong>Specialists</strong>: Even the most experienced must question assumptions, admit uncertainty, and collaborate. The best ones are humble and learn from their mistakes over and over, again and again</li>
<li><strong>Oncologists</strong>: Their thinking blends science with soul. It isn't only about whether a drug shrinks a tumor, but how a patient wants to live the rest of their life</li>
</ul>
<h2>The Outside Pressures</h2>
<p>Groopman doesn't spare the wider system. Pharmaceutical marketing exerts a gravitational pull on prescribing habits. Normal stages of life, like menopause, aging, mild cholesterol, get medicalized, made into conditions requiring lifelong treatment. Doctors are not immune to these influences; they must consciously resist them.</p>
<h2>Patients as Partners</h2>
<p>One of the book's most practical lessons is for patients. You don't have to be passive. Doctors are less likely to fall into cognitive traps when patients and families ask questions like:</p>
<ul>
<li>"What else could it be?"</li>
<li>"Is there anything that doesn't fit?"</li>
<li>"Could I have more than one problem?"</li>
<li>"What's the worst it could be?"</li>
<li>"What's the nearest organ to where I have a problem?"</li>
</ul>
<p>These questions pull doctors back into active thinking, force them to test their assumptions, and can change the course of care.</p>
<h2>The Core Lesson</h2>
<p>Medicine is uncertain. Doctors are human. What separates the great from the merely competent is not perfect knowledge, but self-awareness: remembering mistakes, questioning easy answers, being willing to say "I might be wrong." And what makes care truly effective is partnership—trust, communication, and a willingness for patients and doctors to think together.</p>
<p>Groopman's message is both sobering and hopeful: the mind of a doctor is powerful, but fragile. When patients understand how it works, and how it fails—they can help it work better.</p>
