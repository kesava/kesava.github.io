Devil in the Grove

By Gilbert King

In the wake of the Minnesota protests, and in the middle of Black History Month, I found myself wanting to read a story about what courage looks like when institutions are failing.

Gilbert King’s Devil in the Grove gives us Thurgood Marshall before he becomes Thurgood Marshall, before Brown v. Board of Education, before the Supreme Court. Here, he is a lawyer traveling the back roads of Florida in 1949, entering counties where the rule of law is thin and racial terror feels imminent and ambient.

The book centers on the Groveland case: four young Black men accused of raping a white woman in Lake County, Florida. Evidence is scarce. Emotion is not. Within hours, white mobs burn homes in Black neighborhoods. Families flee into swamps. The sheriff arrests the accused but does little to restrain the violence around them. Order is declared restored.

One of the accused, Ernest Thomas, never reaches a courtroom. A posse hunts him down and shoots him more than four hundred times. It is less an act of law enforcement than a ritual of dominance. And yet the machinery of justice grinds forward.

Into this landscape walks Thurgood Marshall, then chief counsel for the NAACP Legal Defense Fund.

He knows the risks. Black lawyers in the South do not move anonymously. He is warned about driving alone at night. There are quiet suggestions that accidents happen. The NAACP leadership understands that if Marshall is killed, it would be more than a personal tragedy; it would be a strategic loss for a movement still assembling itself.

What is striking is not that Marshall is fearless in the cinematic sense. It is that he is methodical.

He studies the record.

He challenges the composition of all-white juries.

He dissects coerced confessions.

He builds appeals in courtrooms that are structurally hostile.

The initial trials are swift and predictable: convictions, death sentences. But Marshall presses upward, toward federal review. Eventually, the U.S. Supreme Court orders a new trial.

And then, on a rural road, the fragility of that victory becomes clear. Two of the defendants are being transported back to Lake County. The sheriff later claims they attempt to escape. He shoots both men. One dies. The other, Walter Irvin, survives by playing dead, lying still long enough to convince his would-be executioner that the job is finished.

It is a moment that exposes how porous the boundary between legality and violence can be.

Marshall does not withdraw.

He continues the legal fight, not in rhetoric, but in filings, motions, and arguments. His work is slow, procedural, incremental. There is no guarantee of triumph. Only the discipline of showing up again and again.

Marshall’s bravery lies partly in his refusal to romanticize the system. He sees clearly how compromised it is. And yet he insists on engaging it. He believes that the Constitution is not self-executing, that it requires advocates willing to risk comfort, reputation, even safety, to force it toward coherence.

Brown v. Board did not materialize as a singular flash of moral clarity. It was constructed, case by case, in places like Groveland. The discipline Marshall developed here, the attention to record, the insistence on federal oversight, the patience to outlast local resistance, becomes the architecture of later victories.

What stays with me is not only the scale of injustice, but the steadiness of the response. Marshall’s courage is not loud. It is sustained. It is the decision to enter rooms where one is unwelcome and argue, calmly, that the law must mean what it says.

In that sense, Devil in the Grove is less a story about a single case than about formation. There is a lawyer, carrying briefs into hostile counties, choosing persistence over safety.

That feels worth studying.
