18:T1743,<h1>The British in India: A Social History of the Raj</h1>
<p>By David Gilmour</p>
<p>David Gilmour's <em>The British in India</em> begins and ends under the shadow of two queens — Elizabeth I and Elizabeth II. Between them stretches nearly three centuries of British presence in India: an era of adventurers and administrators, memsahibs and merchants, hunters and philologists, soldiers and saints.</p>
<p>Gilmour's ambition is not to rehearse the familiar chronicle of conquest and governance but to render a social history — a portrait of the men and women who lived and died in the subcontinent. He writes with a sensitivity rare in imperial histories: refusing to impose the certainties of the present upon the ambiguities of the past. The result is a richly panoramic work, elegantly written, full of irony and detail, yet unburdened by the jargon or moralizing that often clouds the subject.</p>
<h3><strong>Three Tribes of the Raj</strong></h3>
<p>Khushwant Singh once remarked that there were three kinds of Britons in India: those who detested the place, those who merely did their job, and those who fell helplessly in love with it. Gilmour's gallery contains all three — the irritable district officer who cursed the dust and the smell, the pragmatic engineer who built canals in Madras, and the eccentric linguist who compiled grammars of Telugu and Urdu while sipping warm gin in the evening breeze.</p>
<p>The most striking fact, as Gilmour reminds us, is how few of them there were. Even at the height of the Raj in 1901, there were only about 155,000 Britons — mostly soldiers — among more than 230 million Indians. During the great rebellion of 1857, there were nearly six Indian soldiers for every Briton in the army. They ruled less through sheer force than through a dense network of collaboration. For all their talk of India as a "jewel," most had little desire to live there long; it was enough to know it gleamed somewhere in the imperial vault.</p>
<h3><strong>Aspirations and Motivations</strong></h3>
<p>They came to India for many reasons — to make money, to hunt tigers, to escape debt, or simply to test their mettle in the tropics. Some were younger sons of impoverished gentry; others were restless soldiers of fortune or earnest missionaries. A few were drawn by literature and imagination itself — men who had grown up on Kipling's tales and longed to see the "scents of dust and spices" for themselves.</p>
<p>Unlike later empires driven by ideology, these men were rarely animated by doctrines of destiny. Empire was less a creed than a career — or sometimes an accident, a byproduct of opportunity and curiosity. As Gilmour shows, adventure more than ambition drew many across the seas.</p>
<h3><strong>Endeavors</strong></h3>
<p>Their pursuits were as varied as their temperaments: irrigation, administration, linguistics, law, and mapping. Gilmour writes with affection of the engineers who built dams and canals, such as Sir Arthur Cotton, who dreamed of making Madras province bloom. Alongside them were the botanists collecting Himalayan ferns, the missionaries printing gospels in Bengali, and the surveyors who mapped the subcontinent one contour at a time.</p>
<p>Yet their social world was as strange as it was self-assured. It was a society obsessed with ceremony and etiquette to the edge of absurdity. A newly arrived official in Madras was expected to leave calling cards at every bungalow in town — a process that could take weeks in the heat. Meals were gargantuan: beefsteak and claret for breakfast, followed by turkeys and mutton for supper. At elite clubs, juniors were confined to one end of the bar and allowed to move toward the center only when invited by their seniors. Empire had its rituals, and the rituals sustained the illusion of order.</p>
<h3><strong>Experiences and Private Worlds</strong></h3>
<p>The private life of the Briton in India was rarely tranquil. Few women came from home; those who did found the voyage perilous and the climate unforgiving. Many men lived with <em>bibis</em> — Indian companions who offered domesticity and affection in an alien land — relationships later erased from official memory.</p>
<p>India was also a landscape of release and temptation. Cities such as Bombay were described as "dens of iniquity," where moral boundaries blurred in the tropical heat. Yet even within that chaos, India entered the senses with disarming intimacy. Some fell in love with the <em>godhuli vela</em>, the "cow-dust hour" at dusk, when the air thickened with jasmine and dust. Others awaited the monsoon's first downpour, a relief so profound it felt like grace.</p>
<p>When they finally sailed home, they carried the memory of India as a lingering fragrance — a place of contradictions that had unsettled and transformed them.</p>
<h3><strong>The Bequest</strong></h3>
<p>The book ends not in triumph or apology but in quiet melancholy. The British, Gilmour observes, did not give India its civilization or its soul; yet they left behind institutions that endured — railways, universities, the civil service, and a parliamentary habit that still echoes Westminster.</p>
<p>But the heart of his story lies in individuals rather than systems. One of the final portraits is of Kathleen Myers, a college principal who, after independence, stayed on in Ooty. She spent her final years attending bridge games, cultural circles, and Sunday films — the last custodian of a vanished age. In her small routines, Gilmour captures the strange dignity and sadness of those who outlived the empire they had served.</p>
<p>By restoring these lives to full human scale, he rescues the Raj from abstraction. His narrative is not a defense of empire but an anatomy of experience — an exploration of how ordinary men and women, caught between duty and desire, lived out their days in a land that was never wholly theirs, yet never again quite foreign.</p>
