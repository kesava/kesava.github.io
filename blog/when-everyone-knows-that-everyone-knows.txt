19:T12af,<h1>When Everyone Knows That Everyone Knows</h1>
<p>By Steven Pinker</p>
<p>“Can you pass the salt?” sounds like the most boring sentence in the world. But if you stop and think about it, it’s actually kind of strange.</p>
<p>You’re not really asking whether someone is physically able to lift a salt shaker. You’re politely asking them to hand it to you. And somehow, this indirect question works perfectly. Nobody is confused. Everyone knows exactly what’s going on.</p>
<p>Steven Pinker’s book <em>When Everyone Knows That Everyone Knows</em> is about why things like this work — and why the same hidden logic also explains a lot of bigger, messier human behavior.</p>
<p>The key idea is something called common knowledge. It’s not just what people know. It’s what they know that other people know, and what they know that other people know they know. It sounds brain-twisty, but it quietly runs a huge part of social life.</p>
<p>A classic example is <em>The Emperor’s New Clothes</em>. Everyone can see that the emperor is naked. But nobody says anything. Why? Because even though each person knows the truth, they don’t know that everyone else is ready to admit it too.</p>
<p>The moment a child says it out loud, everything changes. Now it’s public. Now everyone knows that everyone knows. And suddenly, people feel free to react. The facts didn’t change — the social situation did.</p>
<p>This same pattern shows up in real life more than we realize: in offices, in politics, in social movements, and even in friend groups. People often wait for a signal that it’s “safe” to act.</p>
<p>The book also explains something that seems ridiculous but keeps happening anyway: toilet paper shortages during emergencies.</p>
<p>There usually isn’t an actual supply problem. But in the 1970s, a TV joke about a shortage caused people to rush out and buy toilet paper — because they knew millions of others had heard the same joke. Their buying created the shortage.</p>
<p>Over time, that reaction became baked into public thinking. Now “emergency = toilet paper panic” is common knowledge. People aren’t reacting to real shortages — they’re reacting to what they expect everyone else to do.</p>
<p>Pinker also looks at how common knowledge shapes ideas about generosity. He brings in an old ethical framework known as Maimonides’ Ladder of Charity — a medieval ranking of how “good” different kinds of giving are.</p>
<p>At the very top is giving in a way that preserves dignity: quietly helping someone become self-sufficient, without public credit. Lower rungs include public donations, and at the bottom are gifts that humiliate or obligate.</p>
<p>What’s striking is that modern psychology experiments replicate the same ranking almost perfectly.</p>
<p>People consistently rate anonymous giving as more sincere and virtuous than public giving — even when the anonymous gift is smaller. Once a donation becomes public, people start wondering about motives. Was it kindness, or was it branding?</p>
<p>The moment generosity becomes common knowledge, its moral “flavor” changes.</p>
<p>Pinker then makes an even stranger observation: some human signals are valuable precisely because they <em>cannot</em> be faked.</p>
<p>Blushing, laughing, crying, and genuine embarrassment are all involuntary bodily reactions. You can’t reliably turn them on and off at will. And that makes them powerful social proof.</p>
<p>A blush says, <em>I didn’t plan this.</em></p>
<p>A real laugh says, <em>This actually got me.</em></p>
<p>Tears say, <em>This matters to me more than my self-image.</em></p>
<p>These are what Pinker calls credibility signals — visible evidence that bypasses strategy and reputation-management. They instantly create common knowledge about sincerity.</p>
<p>That’s why a politician’s scripted apology often feels hollow, but a choked-up, stumbling one can suddenly feel real. The emotion itself becomes a public, shared proof of authenticity.</p>
<p>The main idea of the book is that human life runs on more than facts. It runs on shared awareness — who knows what, who knows that others know it, and who knows that everyone knows that everyone knows it.</p>
<p>This invisible layer shapes how people speak, when they act, what they buy, how they give, how they apologize, and even how they trust each other.</p>
<p>It explains why indirect language works, why silence can last so long, why social change can feel sudden — and why a single public signal can flip entire groups at once.</p>
<p>Once you notice common knowledge at work, you start seeing it everywhere — at the dinner table, online, in meetings, and in moments when entire societies quietly pivot.</p>
<p>Recommended.</p>
