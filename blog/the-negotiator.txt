18:Te19,<h1>The Negotiator</h1>
<p>By George Mitchell</p>
<p>This book is a big picture memoir of George Mitchell. It gives a pretty good sense of who he is and what made him tick. This is the first biography of a non-head of state I read, and I loved it.</p>
<p>He started out as a federal judge, and get this—he actually turned down a spot on the U.S. Supreme Court because he wanted to get into politics. And that gamble totally paid off.</p>
<p>When Senator Edmund Muskie left to become Secretary of State, Mitchell got appointed to fill his seat temporarily. At the time, he was <em>way</em> behind in the polls—like 36 points. Everyone thought he didn’t stand a chance. But two years later, he ran and won the seat on his own with 61% of the vote.</p>
<p>From there, he shot up fast. After just one full term, he became Senate Majority Leader. Then in his next election, he crushed it—81% of the vote in Maine, which is wild considering the state usually leaned Republican. After two terms, he stepped away from the Senate but kept taking on major roles—like helping broker peace in Northern Ireland.</p>
<p>He was Clinton’s special envoy there and played a big part in the Good Friday Agreement.</p>
<p>His background’s pretty interesting too. His dad was the orphaned son of Irish immigrants and grew up in a Boston orphanage. He got adopted by a non-Irish couple and ended up working as a school janitor in Maine. Mitchell’s mom was Lebanese—she immigrated as a teen, worked night shifts in a textile plant, and somehow still managed to be there for her five kids every morning. George says she was the biggest influence on his life. Classic American dream kind of story.</p>
<p>One thing he says really helped him in politics? Just learning how to listen. Like really listen. He says he didn’t learn much from talking—but listening, reading, observing, that’s where the real learning happened.</p>
<p>And he applied that everywhere—especially in Northern Ireland, where ten different political parties who had basically been at each other’s throats for centuries were supposed to negotiate peace. Mitchell told them, “Look, I come from the U.S. Senate—we debate forever. I can sit here and listen as long as you want to talk.”</p>
<p>And wow, they talked. Months turned into years. Same people, same speeches. Over and over. He says it took every ounce of patience he had, but he listened.</p>
<p>He also talks a lot about risk—how taking chances changed his life. Walking away from the bench, turning down the Supreme Court, jumping into politics when the odds weren’t in his favor. And then again, when he agreed to lead the Northern Ireland talks. The biggest gamble? Setting a hard deadline: midnight, April 10, 1998. That became the Good Friday Agreement. Without that line in the sand, he thinks it would’ve all fallen apart.</p>
<p>He says you have to be open to chance—even when you don’t know what form it’ll take. Sometimes it gives you a way out, a fresh start, or even a whole new life. It did for him.</p>
<p>There’s this powerful moment he shares about a 15-year-old girl named Claire Gallagher. She was blinded in the Omagh bombing and told him, “You have to bring this to an end.” That really hit him. Even after everything she went through, she was thinking of everyone else. He stayed in touch with her over the years, and he and his wife actually named their daughter Claire in her honor.</p>
<p>Anyway, the whole book is him telling his story—and i loved how simple, unassuming and straight to the point he is.</p>
<p>Recommend.</p>
