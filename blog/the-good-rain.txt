18:T125f,<h1>The Good Rain</h1>
<p>By Timothy Egan</p>
<p>Timothy Egan’s love for the Pacific NorthWest region of America is palpable on every single page. His book <em>The Good Rain</em> reads less like a travelogue and more like someone slowly learning how a place works by moving through it, noticing details, and then circling back when those details start connecting. He opens on a personal note, carrying his grandfather’s ashes into the Cascades and trying to scatter them in a landscape that feels vast enough to hold memory without containing it. It sets up the book in a quiet way, grounding everything that follows in something lived rather than observed from a distance.</p>
<p>Any introduction to Pacific Northwest has to start with rain. Lewis and Clark spend months in the region and barely record any dry days, and you start to see that this isn’t just weather in the background but something that shapes everything else, from the density of the forests to the way time stretches in that landscape.</p>
<p>What makes the Pacific Northwest feel unusual is how sharply it contrasts with the rest of the West. Move inland and the land turns dry, dependent on a couple of overworked rivers like the Colorado and the Columbia, while the coastal strip stays lush in a way that feels almost out of place. That imbalance sits underneath a lot of what follows, even when Egan is writing about history or people rather than geography.</p>
<p>The early European arrivals come in with a very specific mindset. They are looking for routes, for trade, for something they can turn into a system. The Columbia River becomes valuable because it might connect the interior to the ocean. The fur trade builds quickly around that idea, and then just as quickly collapses when fashion shifts in Europe and the demand disappears. It leaves behind a familiar trace, where the intensity of the activity far outweighs its durability.</p>
<p>The human cost unfolds alongside that, though Egan doesn’t isolate it as a separate story. Disease moves faster than any organized encounter, and native populations are devastated before they have much chance to respond. It sits there in the narrative as one more consequence of the same pattern.</p>
<p>When he turns to the Olympic Peninsula, the scale shifts. The forests are hard to take in all at once, with trees that feel oversized even by American standards. Sitka spruce, Douglas fir, hemlock, all packed into a landscape that seems to operate on its own terms. It makes sense that someone seeing it for the first time would reach for something like Olympus as a comparison.</p>
<p>There are moments where protection enters the story, like Roosevelt setting aside parts of the peninsula as a national monument, but those moments arrive after logging has already started to reshape the land. When wartime demand for spruce ramps up, the forests are treated less as something singular and more as a supply that happens to meet a need.</p>
<p>The geography itself keeps reminding you that the system is more precise than it looks. The Olympic Mountains pull moisture out of the air on one side, leaving places like Victoria comparatively dry, which feels counterintuitive until you see how the pattern holds.</p>
<p>Seattle grows in a way that feels tied to those constraints rather than separate from them, taking advantage of Puget Sound as a natural harbor rather than forcing a solution where one doesn’t exist. Its reputation for constant rain is slightly misplaced; it’s not especially wet compared to other cities, just persistently gray in a way that stretches the gaps between sunlight long enough for people to forget where they put their sunglasses, which might explain why the city reportedly loses more of them than anywhere else.</p>
<p>The Columbia River pulls the narrative forward into something more recognizably modern. The dam projects reshape the river at a massive scale, making irrigation possible across millions of acres and turning parts of the inland Northwest into productive farmland. At the same time, those changes alter migration routes that had existed for centuries, and the decline of salmon runs follows in a way that feels less like a single mistake and more like something built into the design from the beginning.</p>
<p>What stays with you is not a single argument but a way of seeing the place. The Pacific Northwest remains extraordinary, but it has been shaped by decisions that made sense at the time they were made. Egan has an extraordinary knack in observing details and transport you into the scenery and make you feel alive with the local sounds and breeze and water and snow and dry land.</p>
<p>Recommended.</p>
