19:T1df6,<h1>Deng Xiaoping and the Transformation of China</h1>
<p>by Ezra Vogel</p>
<p>Every once in a while, I stumble into a book that leaves me in a daze of high. The high from being exposed to something that I should have known all along, but never spent any time trying to know about it. The high from learning about a man who is such a colossus of 20th century and being inspired by his life. The high from reading a biography in the same league as Robert Caro's third volume of Lyndon Johnson - Master of the Senate. The high from learning about a key piece of history that enables understanding how other pieces have fallen into place.</p>
<p>I have been a map and general knowledge nerd all my childhood, but I confess I have never spent any substantial time pouring over a map of China. nor did I ever look up a topographical map of China. This book not only forced, but inspired to me learn more about Chinese geography.</p>
<p>I spent last few days finishing this book that is definitely going to be one of the best books I will read this year. The book is both a biography of Deng Xiaoping as well as history of how China transformed into a world power.</p>
<p>Deng Xiaoping's life has more than his fair share of tragedy. Mao exiled him three times. Once, he was sent to a remote village, where he repaired farm machinery. But each time, he found a way back. He did so through self-criticism and political maneuvering. By the time he returned for good in the late 1970s, he was a pragmatist first, an ideologue last.</p>
<p>He believed in results. His famous "black cat, white cat" theory captured this. It didn't matter what the method was, as long as it worked. Whenever he was doing something that was contradicting Mao, He reframed Mao and argued that Mao's words had different meanings at different times. His focus was on moving forward. He prioritized economic growth over ideological purity.</p>
<p>Deng pushed key reforms. He promoted the "Four Modernizations" to modernize industry, agriculture, defense, and science. He reinstated competitive exams for universities, replacing the chaos of Mao's Cultural Revolution with merit-based admissions. He worked with Zhou Enlai in the 1970s and later outmaneuvered Hua Guofeng, Mao's chosen successor.</p>
<p>His travels changed his perspective. He sent his minsters on a travel as well. They visited Yugoslavia, where they saw a looser form of socialism. In Western Europe, they were stunned by openness and efficiency. At Charles de Gaulle Airport, they were surprised by the use of computers. More importantly, they saw that workers were not being exploited, yet their economies thrived. Meanwhile, thousands of Chinese were fleeing to Hong Kong. Deng saw the cause clearly: China lacked opportunity. His answer? Special Economic Zones (SEZs), starting with Shenzhen in 1980.</p>
<p>One of the first reforms was around cloth rationing. He deregulated cloth production to phase out ration coupons, easing people into a freer market. When a commission suggested China borrow $18 billion from the U.S. for equipment, Deng quipped, "Why not 80?" Secretary of State Brzezinski told President Carter: Deng was in a hurry.</p>
<p>The Third Plenum of 1978 marked China's shift to reform. But they avoided giving Deng unchecked power that led to past disasters like the Great Leap Forward. But he didn't seek formal titles. He wanted authority to push change. He introduced the "Four Cardinal Principles" to set ideological boundaries while allowing controlled reform.</p>
<p>His strategy was simple: push change in small steps, let people adjust, then push harder. He used clever arguments to sidestep ideological battles. If a small farmer selling sunflower seeds didn't harm socialism, why not allow hiring a few workers? He knew how to shape the political climate so party officials would follow.</p>
<p>Deng also welcomed foreign investment. SEZs in Guangdong and Fujian flourished because local officials honored contracts and solved problems. Corruption existed, but as long as results came, Deng tolerated it. This worried the Communist Party, but growth took priority. In agriculture, he let peasants take control. The results were dramatic. Grain production surged, and by 1984, China stopped requiring farmers to sell grain to the state. Many switched to cash crops. Some areas kept collectivization, which helped care for the elderly.</p>
<p>Deng's approach mirrored Franklin D. Roosevelt's—experiment, adjust, and expand success. He used food shortages to justify private farming. He used crime to justify allowing small shops.</p>
<p>He had to handle China's territorial disputes carefully. He sought talks with Chiang Ching-kuo, Chiang Kai-shek's son, about Taiwan. In 1984, he negotiated with Margaret Thatcher over Hong Kong's return. He stood firm. The UK had to hand Hong Kong back by 1997. He listened to Hong Kongers' concerns about democracy and tried to ease their fears. Tibet was a harder challenge.</p>
<p>Deng tested two batch of new leaders: Zhao Ziyang and Jiang Zemin. Zhao, a reformist, had a free hand until 1989. But when he sympathized with Tiananmen protesters, he lost Deng's support. Jiang had experience handling student protests in Shanghai. He was more loyal to the Party.</p>
<p>The 1989 Tiananmen protests started with students. Until then, the government assigned them jobs, keeping them under surveillance. By the late 1980s, inflation and corruption angered the public. Protesters filled Tiananmen Square. On May 13, students began hunger strikes. The movement peaked before Gorbachev's visit. The Party failed to negotiate. It refused to share power. Deng believed Soviet and Polish communists had lost control because they acted too late. He chose force.</p>
<p>On June 3-4, troops moved in. It was a dark night, with no moon. Tanks rolled into Beijing. The government restored order. Zhao Ziyang, who had pleaded with students to leave, was placed under house arrest. He remained there until his death in 2005. Jiang Zemin, who took a hard line on protests, became the next leader.</p>
<p>Deng slowly stepped back from power. He set a precedent: retirement before death. But he still influenced policy. He met world leaders, including George H.W. Bush, who had known him since the 1970s. Tiananmen led to Western sanctions. China also faced economic instability after lifting price controls in 1988. Planners took a cautious approach. SEZ expansion slowed.</p>
<p>In 1992, at age 87, Deng made his famous Southern Tour. He visited Shenzhen to see its success. He wanted Shanghai to follow. He continued his tour and nudged the nation to more reforms and SEZs. Jiang Zemin hesitated but later embraced reform. Shanghai SEZ was established. China's economy took off.</p>
<p>Deng spent his final years watching his work bear fruit. In 1997, he died at 92. His reforms shaped modern China. The system he built mostly survived, though later leaders removed term limits.</p>
<p>Mao had centralized power to ensure quick decisions. Deng reversed course. He gave local officials more control—as long as they delivered results. It was like the American Wild West. Officials bent rules to drive growth. Corruption existed, but economic success mattered more.</p>
<p>Ultimately, Deng's reforms unified China in ways unseen since its first unification in 221 BC. His legacy lives on in China's rise as a global power.</p>
<p>China lept their saints (Mao), but shredded their scripture. It kept me wondering if India seemed to have shredded the saints (Nehru), but kept their scripture (India's foreign policy).</p>
<p>At 750 pages, it is highly highly highly recommended.</p>
