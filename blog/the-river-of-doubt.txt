18:T18e1,<h1>The River of Doubt</h1>
<p>By Candice Millard</p>
<p>Over the years, America has been blessed now and then with presidents who were something more than politicians.</p>
<p>Washington, who refused to be king and walked away from power after two terms.</p>
<p>Lincoln, who trudged miles to return a few cents he had overcharged as a young clerk.</p>
<p>FDR, who called plays from his wheelchair like a quarterback and somehow made Churchill feel they were winning every down.</p>
<p>And then there was <strong>Theodore Roosevelt</strong> — the most restless of them all, a man carved out of contradiction and sheer force of will.</p>
<p>Teddy was born a fragile boy, wracked by asthma. Most would have grown up cautious; Roosevelt decided to rebuild himself instead. He "remade" his body through relentless exercise and adventure until action itself became his identity. He ranched cattle in the Dakotas, charged up San Juan Hill with the Rough Riders, and hunted on safari in Africa. His idea of recovery, after tragedy or defeat, was to go somewhere even harder.</p>
<p>When his wife and mother died on the same day, he left his infant daughter with his sister and vanished into the Badlands. When an assassin's bullet struck him during a campaign speech, he finished the speech before seeing a doctor. When he lost the 1912 presidential race to Woodrow Wilson and felt history's spotlight slipping away, he decided—at fifty-five—to plunge into one of the most dangerous places on Earth.</p>
<p>The invitation came from Brazil: an offer to join an Amazon expedition. At first it was supposed to be a comfortable scientific jaunt. But once there, Roosevelt changed plans on a whim—to explore an uncharted tributary of the Amazon that didn't even appear on maps. It would be called <strong>The River of Doubt</strong>.</p>
<p>His Brazilian co-leader was <strong>Colonel Cândido Rondon</strong>, a man of striking contrast to Roosevelt. Born in deep poverty, Rondon had risen through the army and already led telegraph expeditions through the rainforest. Calm where Roosevelt was fiery, disciplined where Roosevelt was impulsive, Rondon believed in treating Indigenous tribes with respect and restraint—decades ahead of his time.</p>
<p>Roosevelt brought along his son Kermit, then working in Brazil and fluent in Portuguese, to "look after his father." Also in the party were the naturalist George Cherrie, Rondon's aide Lyra, a doctor, and sixteen tough laborers called <em>camaradas</em>. It was a mismatched team: brave but poorly organized, their supplies chosen for comfort, not survival.</p>
<p>From the moment they launched their canoes, the expedition seemed cursed. The river was unpredictable, with rapids and waterfalls forcing them to haul their gear through dense, dripping forest. Boats were smashed; food was lost. Insects swarmed every wound. Jaguars prowled the banks. Piranhas filled the water. The air was thick with rot and rain.</p>
<p>Millard's descriptions make you feel the jungle breathing down their necks—alive, hostile, and indifferent. The men starved; the <em>camaradas</em> grew skeletal from fever and malnutrition. They saw no villages but always sensed eyes upon them. Once, arrows killed their dog. They realized they were being watched by unseen tribes who could have annihilated them at will.</p>
<p>The jungle also consumed its own. One man drowned. Another murdered a comrade in a dispute over stolen food and was left behind to face the forest's verdict—death or worse. As one Brazilian later said, <em>the jungle devours all who enter it.</em></p>
<p>Roosevelt himself nearly didn't make it out. A gash on his leg became infected; fever and malaria set in. At one point, delirious and believing he would die, he urged the others to go on without him. Rondon refused. Kermit refused. They carried him instead.</p>
<p>By the end, the former president who once wrestled bears of the political kind was being paddled downriver, skeletal and half-conscious, his great body finally broken by the same will that had driven him into the jungle. Yet somehow, they emerged—barely—into civilization, gaunt, starving, but alive.</p>
<p>Roosevelt never fully recovered. He lectured, wrote, and spoke with his old fire, but the boundless vitality was gone. The Amazon had taken its due. He died five years later, at sixty-one.</p>
<p>Rondon, in contrast, lived on to ninety-two, becoming a national hero in Brazil and earning a Nobel Peace Prize nomination from Albert Einstein.</p>
<p>Kermit's ending was the most tragic. Loyal, capable, and melancholy by nature, he never escaped his father's shadow. After years of drifting, he took his own life at fifty-three.</p>
<p>Candice Millard tells this story with cinematic precision—the sweat, the insects, the moral fatigue of men watching their rations dwindle and their spirits crack. But beneath the jungle adventure lies a portrait of a man who refused to live an ordinary life.</p>
<p>For Roosevelt, the River of Doubt was not just a place; it was a mirror. Every mile downstream stripped away the armor of bravado until what remained was something elemental—his stubborn courage, his need to test himself, his refusal to sit still while life stood waiting. He had built his life around what he called <em>the strenuous life</em>, and here, in the green hell of the Amazon, he lived its final, most punishing sermon.</p>
<p>Millard's book ends quietly, almost elegiacally. Rondon went on to become one of Brazil's national heroes, outliving everyone else and earning a Nobel nomination from Einstein. Kermit, who had steadied his father through the jungle, could not steady himself in life. And Roosevelt, the man of endless motion, finally slowed. He lived five more years, writing, speaking, and still dreaming of distant frontiers. When he died in his sleep at sixty-one, one of his old friends said, "Death had to take Roosevelt sleeping, for if he had been awake, there would have been a fight."</p>
<p>In the long line of American presidents, each extraordinary in his own way, Roosevelt stands apart. Washington had restraint, Lincoln had empathy, FDR had optimism - but Roosevelt had <em>motion.</em> He lived as if movement itself were a form of faith. And so the River of Doubt - wild, perilous, unnamed was the perfect stage for his last great act.</p>
