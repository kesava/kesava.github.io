19:Ta35,<h1>Myths of Light: Eastern Metaphors of the Eternal</h1>
<p>By Joseph Campbell</p>
<p>In Western traditions, Campbell says, we’re used to thinking of truth as something external. God is “up there.” Meaning is “out there.” You search, you find, you believe.</p>
<p>But in the Eastern traditions Hindu, Buddhist, Taoist - he explores that whole structure flips.</p>
<p>The “light” isn’t a distant source you chase. It’s something already present, already shining, just obscured. The metaphors of sun, flame, reflection aren’t pointing to a god outside the world. They’re pointing inward, toward consciousness itself.</p>
<p>One of Campbell’s constant refrains is that myths aren’t meant to be taken literally. They’re not primitive science or bad history. They’re metaphors, they are tools for pointing at experiences that are hard to describe directly.</p>
<p>In Eastern thought, this becomes especially clear. A story about a god, a demon, or a cosmic event isn’t about something that happened once upon a time. It’s about something happening all the time in your mind, your perception, your sense of self.</p>
<p>So when you hear about enlightenment, nirvana, or liberation, Campbell nudges you away from thinking of them as places or achievements. They’re shifts in awareness.</p>
<p>A big thread running through the book is the idea that what we normally experience this sense of “me” as separate from everything else. It is, in a deep sense, a kind of illusion.</p>
<p>Eastern myths keep circling this insight: the self you think you are is only a fragment. Beneath it there’s a more fundamental unity. The same “light” shows up as you, as me, as everything.</p>
<p>That’s why so many Eastern metaphors involve dissolving boundaries as drops merging into the ocean, reflections disappearing when you stop looking at the mirror as separate.</p>
<p>This isn’t just philosophical poetry. Campbell emphasizes that these traditions come with methods such as yoga, meditation, ritual. These are designed to actually <em>experience</em> what the myths point to.</p>
<p>The stories set the direction. The practices do the work.</p>
<p>It’s a bit like being told that music exists so you can understand the idea, but until you listen, or play, it’s abstract. The myths are the sheet music. The practices are the performance.</p>
<p>If you read it slowly, it almost feels less like finishing a book and more like having your mental furniture rearranged. Nothing new has been added, but the space feels different.</p>
<p>Recommended; I need to reread the book.</p>
