Across the Airless Wilds: The Lunar Rover and the Triumph of the Final Moon Landings

by Earl Swift

The joke used to be: Americans are so obsessed with cars, they even drove one on the moon.

It’s funny because it’s true. And because it underplays just how insane that really was.

But the fact is, the giant leap didn’t end with Armstrong’s boot print in the powdery gray. That was the beginning. The “real” moonwalks—the gritty, bouncing, dust-in-the-gears, tire-track kind—came later. Apollo 15, 16, and 17. The final three missions. Each with three astronauts and one weird, folded-up vehicle crammed into the side of the lunar module like an afterthought.

The LRV. The moon buggy. A minimalist aluminum skeleton with wire wheels, no cabin, and a top speed just fast enough to get you killed if something went wrong. And it went far—farther from Earth than any other human-driven vehicle before or since.

Swift’s book isn’t about Apollo 11 and the triumphal headlines. It’s about what came after. The part where exploration meant risk, not ceremony. Where NASA engineers had to shove a working car into a cargo space the size of a dishwasher. Where duct tape had to save the day.

It’s the story most people don’t know—the engineers, dreamers, improvisers, and bureaucratic cat-herders who somehow built a car that could survive a place without roads, without air, without margin for error.

By the time Apollo 14 wrapped up, NASA knew: if they wanted science, they needed range. And to get range, they needed wheels.

Armstrong and Aldrin had covered maybe 100 meters from their lander. Not for lack of curiosity—they were simply anchored by spacesuits that were too heavy to walk around more than a few feet. Life support strapped to their backs, boots with almost no traction. The surface itself was no place for a casual hike.

The moonwalkers needed range. They needed to move faster, farther. They needed a car.

But building a car for the moon? No one even knew what the ground was made of. Before Apollo 11, some engineers thought it might be like talcum powder. One team literally filled a sandbox with flour and tried to drive through it. Others worried the landers might sink.

Even when lunar dust samples arrived, the design challenge remained absurd. There’s no atmosphere, so no air cooling. Gravity’s one-sixth of Earth, so traction is weird. There’s no suspension system that works in a vacuum. Every pound of weight costs a fortune. And whatever they built had to fold up like origami and fit into a space the size of a dishwasher.

Jetpacks, pogo sticks, weird bouncing machines—they were all pitched. But in the end, the idea that won was the most terrestrial: a car. Sort of.

GM and Boeing teamed up. One group led by Sam Romano, smooth and relentless. The other by Ferenc Pavlics, a refugee from Soviet Hungary who had an intuitive feel for all-terrain design. Together, they had 17 months and a ticking clock. The result would weigh 460 pounds, fold like origami, and survive a world that had never known wind.

The timeline was brutal. Seventeen months, thirty-eight million dollars. Constant oversight from every NASA center imaginable. NASA had opinions. Huntsville had opinions. Boeing had committees. GM had lawyers. Everyone had turf to defend and deadlines to miss. The budget ballooned. The design morphed.

Yet somehow, it happened.

The rover’s chassis was ultralight aluminum. The wheels looked like giant tea strainers—steel mesh over titanium ribs, designed to flex and grab. Each one had its own electric motor. No shocks. No steering column. Just cables and wishful thinking.

There were no spares. No backups. No Plan B.

But it worked.

On July 31, 1971, Dave Scott climbed down from Apollo 15’s lander, unfolded the LRV, and started the engine. It rattled. It bounced. It fought the terrain. But it moved. And with that, the moon suddenly grew bigger.

In Earth terms, the distances weren’t huge—17 miles for Apollo 15, 16 for Apollo 16, almost 23 for Apollo 17. But in moon terms, these were epic treks. No GPS. No rescue. Just line of sight to the lander and a cautious eye on the oxygen gauge.

They drove across fields of dust. Up ridges. Down into craters. Sometimes a mile or more from their lander, which, if the rover broke, might as well have been across the ocean.

And yet, the rover held up. Mostly. A fender broke on Apollo 17. Moon dust started coating everything. The fix? A laminated map, duct tape, and some clamps. It worked.

These weren’t joyrides. They were extraction missions. The crews collected hundreds of pounds of rock—some older than Earth’s oceans. The data they brought back reshaped how we understand not just the moon, but planetary formation itself.

And all of it happened with three men in metal suits, riding a motorized deck chair.

Swift doesn’t just tell a tech story. He tells a people story.

Ferenc Pavlics, the soft-spoken engineer who cobbled together working prototypes in parking lots with lawnmower parts. Sam Romano, half diplomat, half field general, who kept the program alive through every setback. Wernher von Braun, looming in the background, dreaming of lunar buses but settling for the world’s first space jalopy.

And the astronauts themselves: Dave Scott, John Young, Gene Cernan. All test pilots. All geologists, at least by crash course. Men trained to stay calm while bouncing across a crater floor at 8 miles per hour, knowing they were one snapped cable away from a slow death.

They had drills. Routines. Limits. They were told not to go beyond a certain walking distance from the lander, just in case. But they pushed it. And every mile gave us more science than every mission before.

The Lunar Rover was the last real expansion of the Apollo program’s ambition. It turned the moon from a target into a terrain.

And then it ended.

After Apollo 17, the program died. Budget cuts. Political fatigue. America had been to the moon, checked the box, moved on. No one ever drove on the moon again.

The tracks are still etched into the dust. No rain to wash them away. No wind to erase the path. Fifty years later, they’re still there. You could follow them from orbit, and you’d know exactly where humans once roamed.

That’s what Across the Airless Wilds captures—not the drama of first contact, but the quiet glory of second steps.

Highly recommended, if you’re an engineering nerd.
