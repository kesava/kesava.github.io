# The Age of Wood

By Roland Ennos

In the beginning, there were trees. Before fire, before flint, before language even, our ancestors clung to wood. Primate bodies evolved in its architecture: hands that grasp, eyes that judge depth, muscles that learned to swing, leap, and land. The forest canopy didn't just shelter us; it shaped us.

Roland Ennos begins his sweeping story here, in the branches. Not with axes or cathedrals, but with intuition. Monkeys, apes, and early humans knew wood not as material, but as environment. It bent, swayed, held weight. It cracked under strain. Knowing wood was survival.

From that early bond, Ennos walks us forward. He argues that human civilization owes more to wood than to stone, metal, or fire. Fire itself, after all, begins with wood. It cooks food, reshapes nutrition, and fuels metal-smelting. Without logs to burn, there is no bronze, no iron, no industry.

As societies advanced, wood became structure. It carried roofs, spanned rivers, launched ships. Great wooden domes, often hidden under stone exteriors, shaped the silhouette of Europe — from the Gothic vaults of cathedrals to the soaring Italian domes and even Asia's hollowed rock shrines, mimicking the timber halls they replaced.

But as civilizations matured, so did their restrictions. Guilds and castes monopolized technique and trade. Knowledge stayed trapped in silos, cross-pollination stifled. Innovation slowed. Timber continued to build the world, but it did so under rules — the wisdom of the few, the work of the many.

Industrialization cracked that mold. Ennos points to figures like Isambard Kingdom Brunel, whose 19th-century block-making factory used steam-powered machines to mass-produce naval pulleys. Suddenly, wood wasn't just hewn — it was engineered. The transformation from artisan joinery to mechanized production marked a turning point.

Yet for most of history, humans built without nails. For over a dozen chapters, Ennos marvels at the ingenuity of joinery — mortise and tenon, dovetails, wooden pegs that held beams together like puzzle pieces. It's only late in the story that iron nails become common. Until then, wood spoke its own language of compression, tension, and fit.

That language still echoes in today's architecture. IKEA may seem a flat-pack abstraction, but it's a descendant of ancient tradition: modular boards, precise joins, functional forms. And in timber skyscrapers and museums like the Centre Pompidou in Metz, cross-laminated wood returns not just as heritage, but as future which is fireproof, carbon-efficient, and light.

Ennos doesn't just chart the rise of a material. He tells a parallel history: of energy, biology, architecture, and society — all underwritten by forests. His story is layered, like growth rings in a trunk. From primate leaps to laminated towers, wood has been not just our tool, but our partner.

A pretty insightful read.
