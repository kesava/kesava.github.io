# Empire of Cotton

By Sven Beckert

Think of 1970s bollywood movie setup: Amitabh Bachchan as the angry young man taking on a rich mill owner. That conflict feels familiar. Sven Beckert’s *Empire of Cotton* is basically the long, global backstory to that tension.

His main idea is simple: the cotton industry didn’t grow just through markets or innovation. It grew through what he calls “war capitalism - using state power, coercion, and sometimes outright violence to control land, labor, and trade.

Cotton grew almost everywhere except Europe. In India, it was part of a balanced system: farmers grew cotton alongside food, spun it locally, and met their own clothing needs. There was trade, even with Rome, but it felt like an extension of surplus, not a system built for scale or domination.

That changes with the new sea routes opened by Christopher Columbus voyages and Vasco da Gama voyage to India. From 1600 to 1750, Indian cotton textiles dominate global markets (The book doesn't quite explain why suddenly). Europeans are still outsiders, operating from port cities, placing orders through baniya networks who are deeply tied to weavers. The system is competitive. European buyers compete with each other, and Indian producers benefit.

Then the balance shifts. Better ships, gunpowder, and navigation let Europeans take over the trade routes themselves. Trade expands, but control consolidates.Europeans realized that if you combine trade with coercion, you can drive prices down.

After the Battle of Plassey, Bengal becomes the testing ground. The British replace baniyas with village agents who supervise weavers. Contracts become restrictive. Weavers lose the ability to choose buyers or set prices. They go from independent producers to something closer to wage labor.

At the same time, Europe shuts Indian textiles out with tariffs and bans, while studying and copying Indian techniques. In Manchester, cotton manufacturing takes off. What’s new here is not just machines, but geography: the raw material is no longer local. Cotton has to be pulled in from across the world.

Early supplies come from places like India and Anatolia, but they aren’t enough. Caribbean plantations, especially in Haiti, expand production. The East India Company hesitates to turn India into a raw cotton supplier, worried about losing textile exports. Indian land systems also make rapid expansion difficult.

So production shifts again. After disruptions in the Caribbean, the United States becomes the main supplier. It has three things others don’t: land, labor, and capital, all in large supply. Cotton output scales up dramatically, built on slavery.

This is where Beckert’s central idea comes in: “war capitalism.” The cotton economy isn’t just markets and machines. It depends on force. During the American Civil War, British and French industrial interests, especially in Liverpool, back the Confederacy. Their argument is blunt: without slavery, the world cannot produce enough cotton.

When the war disrupts supply, other regions step in. India sees a surge in prices and production, even though its cotton isn’t ideal for machines. Egypt produces high-quality cotton and does well for a time.

After the war, the system resets. American production returns. Freed Black farmers resist pure cotton farming, negotiating for food alongside cash crops. In India, the opposite happens. Colonial policies push farmers into cotton monoculture. Credit flows only if they grow cotton. When American cotton comes back in the 1870s, better suited for machines, Indian prices collapse. Farmers who had stopped growing food face famine. Weavers, already displaced by machines, end up as day laborers.

Beckert repeats this pattern across regions. Egypt, Uzbekistan, Mexico, Japan, China. In each case, the state pushes cotton production, often coercively.

Cotton also sits at the center of the Industrial Revolution. It is the first industry where machines are applied at scale to production and processing. That combination of global sourcing and mechanized production becomes the model for modern industry.

Even politics got tied into this. Mahatma Gandhi promoted hand-spinning as part of the *swadeshi* movement. But Indian mill owners supported him mainly because they wanted less competition from British imports, not because they believed in home-spinning.

The book stays focused on power: how states, violence, and coercion made this system possible. It doesn’t spend much time on how cheaper cotton changed everyday life for consumers, or on the technical side of industrialization.

Overall, the book shows how a global industry was built by linking farms, factories, and markets across continent - with a lot of state power shaping the outcome.
