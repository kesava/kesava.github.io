# Culture: The Story of Us, from Cave Art to K-Pop

By Martin Puchner

The book is a tour of how culture gets made, preserved, and passed down. He takes us from ancient cave paintings to TikTok trends, showing that culture isn't just something we inherit—it's something we have to actively keep alive.

If we stop telling stories, stop writing things down, or stop adapting ideas, culture disappears.

One of the biggest points he makes over and over is that nothing is ever truly original. Every idea, every artistic style, every belief system is influenced by what came before. The so-called "golden ages" of culture, like the Renaissance or the rise of Hollywood, only happened because different cultures mixed, borrowed, and reinvented each other's ideas. The author argues impressively that there's no such thing as a "pure" culture—everything we think of as traditional was once a remix of something older.

Throughout the book, he tells fascinating stories that prove this point. Ancient Egyptians under Nefertiti and Akhenaten briefly tried monotheism, and some historians think that may have influenced Moses and Judaism.

Greek theater wasn't just for fun—it was how Athenians debated democracy and politics, making it an early version of today's satire and political commentary.

In India, King Ashoka was one of the first rulers to use writing for official messages, but ironically, his scripts were lost for centuries, while oral traditions survived better. Buddhism, which started in India, ended up thriving more in China and Japan because different cultures reshaped it to fit their own beliefs.

I liked the story about Charlemagne. He wasn't a scholar himself, but he was a huge supporter of education and learning. European manuscripts were written in dense, hard-to-read scripts with no spaces between words, making reading and copying texts difficult. Charlemagne, wanting to improve literacy and scholarship in his empire, tasked monks and scholars with standardizing handwriting.

The result was a new font - "Carolingian minuscule", which introduced: Clear, rounded letters with distinct spacing, Lowercase letters, which made reading and writing more efficient, Spaces between words, a major improvement over earlier scripts, Standardized punctuation, improving readability.

The book's main argument is that culture survives through change, not preservation. When people try to freeze traditions in place, they end up killing them. The things that last—religions, art, storytelling—only survive because people adapt them, remix them, and pass them along in new ways.
