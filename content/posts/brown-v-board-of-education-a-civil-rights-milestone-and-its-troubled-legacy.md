# Brown V. Board of Education: A Civil Rights Milestone and Its Troubled Legacy

By James T. Patterson

As part of 250th anniversary of American Independence readings, I had two goals picking up this book. 

a) I want to read more about the role Thurgood Marshall played in *Brown v. Education*. 

b) *Brown v. Education* is no less than a reconsecration of the ideals outlined in the declaration of independence.

While (b) is definitely true, this book and the whole *Brown v. Education* seems far less about Thurgood Marshall than I imagined. It reads less like a victory lap and more like a story about how a clean legal idea runs into a very messy country.

It starts with Kenneth and Mamie Clark. They are famous for their doll experiments. Black kids, when asked, often picked the white doll as the “good” one and the black doll as the “bad” one. It wasn’t about toys—it was about what segregation was quietly teaching them. That finding ends up in the Supreme Court’s opinion, tucked away in a footnote, a famous footnote called "Footnote 11". It gives the justices something earlier courts didn’t really have: a way to say segregation causes harm even if the buildings and textbooks look “equal.”

That’s important because for decades the law had said the opposite. Going back to Plessy v. Ferguson, the idea was that “separate but equal” was perfectly fine. And this wasn’t just a Southern thing. Courts across the country had upheld segregation in one form or another. By the time *Brown* shows up in 1952, segregation is baked into law in a big chunk of the country, and most voters aren’t exactly rushing to change it.

What Thurgood Marshall and the NAACP do is shift the ground of the argument. At first, they pursue a line of argument that making states enforce Black and white schools equal, is so expensive that it collapses the system. Marshall eventually drops that. He goes for something cleaner: separation itself is the inequality. The Clark studies help make that argument stick.

Then there’s Earl Warren. Earl Warren was a governor of California who helped Dwight Eisenhower get California vote during his 1951 election. Eisenhower appoints him Chief Justice, but deeply regrets later as he wasn’t prepared for the sweeping judgements Warren’s courts make for many many years to come. Patterson attributes much of Brown v. Education to Earl Warren. The Court wasn’t unified until Warren was Chief Justice. Warren shows up, works the room, and pushes for a unanimous decision. That matters because the Court doesn’t have an army to enforce their judgements. All they have is the force of persuasion. On something this volatile, a split decision would’ve invited resistance from day one.

So in 1954, they pronounce a monumental decision - Brown v. Board of Education. The Court says segregation in public schools is unconstitutional, even though many states have democratically institutionalized segregation. It’s a direct rejection of *Plessy*’s logic.

Up until here, it feels like a turning point. Its such a turning point that when Marshall, who was later appointed as a justice by President Lyndon Johnson, died in 1993 - his body was laid to rest in Supreme court and thousands of people come to visit him and pay sobbing tributes for his role in turning the tide. But Patterson’s whole point is that the real story starts after this monumental judgement.

In the South, not much happens at first. A decade later, almost no Black kids are in integrated schools. It takes the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and actual federal pressure to move things. Once that happens, integration in the South ramps up pretty quickly.

But outside the South, things get more complicated. You don’t have laws forcing segregation, but you still end up with segregated schools, because of where people live, how districts are drawn, and how communities organize themselves. The Court tries to push further. They take up cases approving things like busing, but that’s where public support starts to crack. Busing becomes the symbol of overreach, even though plenty of kids were already taking buses for ordinary reasons.

Patterson frames this as a deeper tension. Equality sounds straightforward, but when you try to enforce it, you run into something else people care about: community. Neighborhood schools, local control, not having your kid sent across town. These aren’t trivial concerns.

He also spends time contrasting Marshall with Martin Luther King Jr.. Marshall is the lawyer—incremental, strategic, working case by case. King is operating in a different register—moral language, big appeals, willing to create pressure outside the system. Marshall changes the law; King changes what people are willing to accept. They’re aligned on integration, but not really on method or even temperament.

By the end, Patterson is pretty clear-eyed about the outcome. *Brown* redefined equality—it said it’s not enough for things to look equal on paper, people actually have to share space. That’s a big shift. But turning that into reality runs into limits the Court can’t easily push past. Wealth segregation, housing patterns, and just plain preference end up doing a lot of the sorting.

Patterson tells the story very compellingly. He concludes *Brown* is both a breakthrough and the start of a long argument. It changes the legal definition of equality in a fundamental way. But it also exposes how hard it is to make that definition real, especially when it bumps into how people actually live.

Loved the book.
