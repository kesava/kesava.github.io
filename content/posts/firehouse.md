# Firehouse

By David Halberstam

David Halberstam’s *Firehouse* begins with a line that feels half joke, half truth: *if firefighting were easy, the police would have done it.* He uses that bit of humor to ease us into a world with its own rules, rhythms, and fierce pride. In this world, when the cops put away a criminal, the guy is often back on the street in a week. When firefighters put out a fire, the fire stays put. That clear, no-nonsense sense of accomplishment is part of what draws generations of families into the job. Fathers do it, uncles do it, sons grow up watching them, and sooner or later the sons want to feel that same purpose for themselves.

Halberstam writes about Firehouse 40/35, which is Engine 40 and Ladder 35, in midtown Manhattan. At first glance it is just another neighborhood firehouse, with the usual split between the engine crews and the ladder crews. Engines bring water and muscle. Ladders bring height, rescue, and finesse. Inside, though, the culture is almost monastic. You never wait to be told to do something. You do it because that is how you show loyalty. These are big, strong men who enforce a strict code on themselves and on each other, not out of show, but because the unit only works if everyone trusts everyone else completely. You are expected, in the most literal way, to be willing to give your life for the guy next to you. Most days that code feels like an extended family. They help each other outside work, watch each other’s kids, and pass down small lessons that live outside any official manual.

Then comes September 11.

At 9:30 that morning, both trucks left the firehouse. Thirteen men rode toward the burning towers. They entered the South Tower only ten minutes before it collapsed. One man survived. His name was Shea. His survival was almost accidental. The force of the collapse threw him half a block. He woke with a broken neck, a concussion, and injuries so severe that even walking was hard. When he later spoke honestly about survivor’s guilt, a newspaper branded him a coward. Halberstam lets that injustice sit in the reader’s mind, without melodrama, and it lands even harder because of his restraint.

The book is not really about the run to the towers. Very little is known about it. Shea remembers almost nothing, and the radio system failed badly. Instead, Halberstam writes about the men themselves. Their humor, their pride, the way they treated their families, and the quiet nobility of their daily routines. Two were probies. One was engaged. One was filling in from another house on his very first day. Several were not even supposed to be on shift but had swapped for overtime or to help a friend. Some were close to retirement. All of them were the kind of people you hope firefighters are: hardworking, honest, and willing to put other people first.

Reading the book, you feel inspired by these men. You also feel a little smaller in comparison. Their generosity, their steadiness, the way they handled danger and relationships and responsibility feels extraordinary. Halberstam uses a calm, simple voice, which only makes the emotional weight stronger. This is not a sweeping 9/11 history. It is a modest, beautiful, and necessary tribute.

Captain Callahan’s funeral was the last one from the firehouse. Captains, as everyone in the department knows, go in first and come out last. In a way, that single fact explains why 40/35 mattered so much to each other, and why the loss still echoes so clearly. 

The book is short, but it stays with you for a long time. Tears roll down profusely as book ends with fathers eulogizing their sons, wives mourning their husbands, friends celebrating their heroic friends.

Highly recommended.
