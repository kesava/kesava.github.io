# Brunelleschi's Dome

**By Ross King**

Florence in the 13th and 14th centuries was riding a building boom fueled by the wool trade. Towers climbed higher, churches spread wider, and the city decided its cathedral, Santa Maria del Fiore, would be the largest in Europe. The design called for a dome so big it would outshine anything since antiquity.

## The Impossible Challenge

They built everything but the dome. For more than fifty years the great circular opening sat exposed, a reminder of Florence's ambition and its engineering ignorance. Other cities laughed—Florence had given itself a roof it couldn't finish.

The Opera del Duomo—the Works Department—finally called for a competition. Filippo Brunelleschi won. Years earlier, he had entered another famous contest, for the bronze doors of the Baptistery across from the cathedral. He tied with Lorenzo Ghiberti, but when offered a shared commission, Brunelleschi's pride got the better of him. He walked away and went to Rome.

## Learning from the Ancients

With Donatello as companion, he spent years among the ruins, studying how the ancient Romans built—how they shaped stone to lock into arches, how they made vaults and domes stand without collapse, and how they moved impossible weights.

An arch, he knew, is simple in theory—two vertical supports and a curved span made of wedge-shaped stones, each pressing on the next. But it needs wooden scaffolding until the keystone locks in place. Repeat that arch in a circle and you have a dome. For centuries, domes had been built the same way: fill the space beneath with a forest of wooden centering until the last stone is set and mortared.

Florence's dome was so large that the centering would have required trees the size of redwoods—trees nobody in Europe even knew existed. Brunelleschi claimed he could build it without centering. It was audacious enough to win him the job.

## Innovation Upon Innovation

There were other problems. The dome would rise 400 feet. How to lift massive stone beams and thousands of bricks into place? How to move workers up and down daily? Brunelleschi invented a hoist powered by oxen that could raise 1,000-pound loads in under fifteen minutes and reverse direction without unyoking the animals—essentially a primitive crane.

The self-supporting brickwork was another puzzle. Brunelleschi solved it with a herringbone pattern that let each brick support the next before mortar set. He cinched the structure with belts of stone and iron, locking it together as it rose. He even worked out how to transport stone from distant quarries to the job site, floating loads down the Arno.

## The Legacy

The project took sixteen years, through wars, shortages, and political intrigue, but the dome was finished—a double shell of masonry, elegant and immense. Florence had its crown. Brunelleschi had revived Roman engineering and improved it, sparking a turn in European architecture from Gothic spires to the graceful curves of domes.

Michelangelo studied the dome and vowed to outdo it with St. Peter's in Rome. He didn't. His was smaller, heavier, less refined. Centuries later, the United States Congress copied the form for the Capitol, only to build a smaller one yet again.

## Verdict

Ross King's *Brunelleschi's Dome* is equal parts engineering puzzle, political theater, and cultural history. It's the story of a city's most audacious project, a man who refused to share credit, and the masterpiece that became the Renaissance's opening act.

Such a wonderful book, if you are an engineering or architecture geek.
