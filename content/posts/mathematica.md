# Mathematica

By David Bessis

There's a small exercise David Bessis likes to begin with.

Tie your shoelaces—something you've done a million times.

Now try writing down the instructions for someone who has never even seen a shoe. Not just the broad steps, but every twist, every fold of lace, the precise choreography of fingers. Very quickly the simple act becomes impossibly baroque. The more meticulously you explain, the more absurd the instructions start to look.

Bessis says: that's what mathematical proofs often feel like.

The written trace of a very human gesture.

Now imagine someone showing you how to tie that lace. Suddenly the whole thing is obvious. "Oh—that's all it is?"

According to Bessis, mathematics works the same way. With intuition, the act is simple—even beautiful. Without it, the instructions look like foreign machinery. And so he urges readers: when math feels needlessly complex, assume the problem is the lack of a picture in your mind, not a lack of talent. There is no special math gene. Not even for Ramanujan. What we call "talent" is often just the early formation of intuition.

Bessis's central message is simple:

**mathematics is an act of seeing.**

Not memorizing. Not grinding through steps. Seeing.

### **Reading Between the Lines**

In *Mathematica*, Bessis argues that mathematical texts are not meant to be read like novels—or worse, like robots marching from line 1 to line 300.

What matters is "the thoughts between the lines": the internal film playing in the mathematician's mind. The part that never fully makes it onto the page. A proof is a fossil; the living creature was intuitive.

And intuition, he says, is the true creative work of mathematicians—more than the papers they publish. It's what they spend their lives building. It's what lets them *live inside* the very objects they study, especially in fields like geometry and topology. Bessis quotes Bill Thurston's radical idea that understanding means finding a way to *inhabit* a mathematical space, the way a surgeon learns to feel the contour of an organ beneath the fingers.

### **The Moral of a Truth**

One of Bessis's most intriguing notions is that to "understand something morally" means to know *why* it's true, in a way that transcends formal logic.

Logic alone cannot deliver a moral.

Only intuition can.

This is where Bessis turns almost philosophical—mathematics, he insists, is a kind of spiritual awakening. There is a bodily sensation, a clarity, that guides us along the path of knowledge. You don't truly understand Descartes' "clear and distinct" ideas until you've felt this click of intuition yourself. Before that, the words just pass over you like mist.

This clarity, however, requires courage. "Fearing error and fearing truth," Bessis writes, "are the same thing."

If you're afraid to be wrong, discovery shuts down.

### **Elephantitude**

One of the book's more playful sections is titled *Le concept d'éléphantitude*—the idea of "elephantness." Bessis points out something absurdly obvious yet philosophically bothersome: we cannot give a rigorous definition of an elephant, yet a three-year-old can point one out instantly. Intuition beats rationality by several laps.

He flirts with the analogy to deep learning, which can detect elephants without knowing what an elephant *is*. But he uses it not as a technical claim but as a metaphor—the best one he knows—for how human concepts form. Deep learning isn't understanding, but it hints at the machinery of understanding.

For Bessis, mathematics is not about constructing definitions; it's about expanding the reach of our intuition—awakening new chambers of understanding. "The mathematics you understand augment reality," he writes. "They make you hyper-lucid."

### **System 3: Reprogramming Your Intuition**

Borrowing the language of Daniel Kahneman, Bessis proposes a third mental system - neither instinctive (System 1) nor analytical (System 2), but intuitive in a creative, plastic way. A system you can *train*.

He gives practical guidelines:

1. **You can reprogram your intuition.** It isn't fixed; it grows like muscle.
2. **Misalignment between intuition and logic is fertile soil.** That discomfort is where new understanding is born.
3. **Give it time.** Intuitions take shape organically; neurons reconnect at their own pace.
4. **Start with what feels easy.** Tinker, scribble, play. Interpret your own calculations visually or viscerally.
5. **Progress feels invisible—until one day it doesn't.** Then the answer clicks, and it's hard to imagine not seeing it before.

These steps may sound soft, almost artistic—but Bessis insists they are the real work of mathematics. Not the grinding, not the memorization, but the slow composting of understanding until a new intuition blooms.

At its heart, *Mathematica* is not a technical book. It's a humane one.

Bessis wants mathematics to feel accessible, physical, alive—like learning to tie your shoelaces by watching someone's hands instead of memorizing a page of formal steps.

And his larger claim is bold:

**Mathematics is the science of imagination.**
